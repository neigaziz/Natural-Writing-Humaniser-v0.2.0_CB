# Document 06 — Academic writing, authorial voice, and discourse analysis

## Purpose

Provide the discourse-level reference needed for academic editing, especially stance, engagement, citation, rhetorical moves, and authorial voice.

## Metadiscourse

Metadiscourse is commentary by which a producer helps readers organise, interpret, evaluate, and relate to a text [R21]. It includes interactive resources such as transitions, frame markers, endophoric references, evidentials, and code glosses, and interactional resources such as hedges, boosters, attitude markers, self-mention, and engagement. The categories are analytical aids, not rigid word lists. A form such as “therefore” can connect propositions in the world or organise an argument; “possible” can hedge a claim or describe real-world possibility [R21].

**Operational consequence:** inspect function in context and across neighbouring clauses before editing.

## Stance and engagement

Academic writers are not necessarily “author evacuated.” They position themselves toward propositions, sources, and readers through degrees of certainty, evaluation, agreement, distance, and anticipation [R21–R24]. Disciplinary and diachronic variation is substantial: a phrase acceptable in social-science argumentation may be inappropriate in a highly compressed experimental Results section [R22, R23].

**Editing rules:**

- preserve the original commitment level unless evidence or the user’s stated aim requires adjustment;
- do not turn “suggests” into “proves”;
- do not weaken a well-supported result by adding generic hedges;
- retain first-person positioning when it is appropriate and present in the source;
- add authorial presence only when the source argument entails it and the genre supports it.

## Citation and reporting verbs

Citations connect the current text with a discourse community and can signal support, contrast, definition, method, background, critique, or alignment. Nesi’s BAWE analysis shows that student citation practices vary by discipline, level, and assignment type [R32]. Jurgens et al. demonstrate that citation frames encode rhetorical function and are related to how scientific contributions are represented [R42].

**Editing rules:** preserve citations, author names, years, and source identity. Change reporting verbs only when the intended source relationship is clear and the change preserves stance. If source support is unknown, flag the issue rather than inventing a stronger relation.

## Rhetorical moves and sections

Research writing commonly performs moves such as establishing a research territory, identifying a gap, presenting an approach, reporting results, interpreting findings, discussing limitations, and stating implications. These moves are not universal fixed templates. BAWE genre families and multidimensional analyses demonstrate variation by discipline, level, and assignment type [R28, R29]. Thesis and journal-article introductions also differ in previewing and authorial presence [R31].

**Section guidance:**

- **Introduction/literature review:** connect prior work to a question or gap; preserve distinctions among summary, synthesis, critique, and the writer’s contribution.
- **Methods:** prioritise procedural precision, sequence, variables, sampling, models, and reproducibility; avoid decorative rhetorical variation.
- **Results:** distinguish observation from interpretation; preserve numbers, uncertainty, and statistical terminology.
- **Discussion:** connect results to claims, mechanisms, prior evidence, limitations, and implications; do not introduce unsupported causal explanations.
- **Proposal:** distinguish planned work, rationale, hypotheses, and expected outcomes from established findings.

## Authorial voice

Voice is a pattern of choices through which a writer expresses perspective, evaluation, identity, and relationship to the discourse community [R24, R35]. It is not a decorative layer added after grammar, and it is not synonymous with informal personal writing.

**Voice-preservation checklist:**

1. What stance does the original writer take?
2. Does the writer use first person, passive constructions, or impersonal framing deliberately?
3. Which terms and sentence patterns recur as legitimate preferences?
4. Does the revision make the text sound like a generic institutional voice?
5. Has any experience, opinion, certainty, or criticism been added?

## Limitations

Discourse categories require interpretation and can be coded differently by analysts. Corpus frequencies cannot determine whether an individual sentence is appropriate. Without the cited sources, source–claim accuracy cannot be fully verified.
