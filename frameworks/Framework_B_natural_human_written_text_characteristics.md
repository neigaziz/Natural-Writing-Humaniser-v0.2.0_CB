# Framework B — Natural human-written text characteristics

**Version:** 0.1.0  
**Status:** Evidence-based working framework for quality-oriented editing.  
**Core warning:** Human writing is not a single style. Naturalness is an interaction between writer, purpose, audience, discipline, genre, proficiency, and revision history.

## B1. Definition of the target

For this project, **natural human-written text** means writing that is intelligible, purposeful, contextually appropriate, rhetorically motivated, and recognisably shaped by a writer’s intended meaning and audience. It does not mean informal, error-filled, idiosyncratic, maximally diverse, or statistically unlike model output. Published academic writing is often conventional, formulaic, dense, and highly edited; these properties can be natural within the relevant community [R25–R31, R36–R37].

## B2. Evidence-based characteristics

| Characteristic | Scientific definition and explanation | Empirical/corpus support and conditions | Limitations and contradictions | Conservative editorial rule |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Context-appropriate lexical choice** | Words are selected for semantic precision, disciplinary fit, register, collocation, and the writer’s intended claim rather than for novelty. | Corpus work shows disciplinary and genre differences in lexical bundles and academic language; academic-language skill extends beyond vocabulary size [R25–R27, R38]. | Frequent vocabulary is not automatically vague; uncommon vocabulary is not automatically precise. L2 writers may use valid community-specific patterns. | Replace a word only when the current choice is inaccurate, awkward in context, unnecessarily inflated, or collocationally weak. |
| **2. Conventional but functional formulaicity** | Recurrent multiword sequences organise discourse, express stance, frame evidence, or signal genre moves. | Lexical-bundle studies document extensive bundles in published and student writing, with disciplinary and proficiency variation [R25–R27]. | A phrase may be conventional and still be the best local choice; avoiding all repetition produces unnatural prose. | Ask what the phrase does. Preserve functional bundles; revise only redundant or generic sequences. |
| **3. Lexical variation with controlled repetition** | Human writers alternate lexical forms when meaning allows, while repeating key terms to maintain reference and precision. | Corpus and discourse research treats variation as conditioned by genre, discipline, and discourse function, not as random diversity [R25–R29]. | Excessive synonymisation can change technical meaning or create false distinctions. | Preserve central terms; vary peripheral wording only when reference remains stable and readability improves. |
| **4. Meaningful syntactic variation** | Writers combine coordination, subordination, nominalisation, passives, actives, and clause-level packaging to express relations and manage information. | Biber and Gray challenge simplistic stereotypes about academic complexity; Biber et al. show that academic writing should not be measured by conversational complexity alone [R36–R37]. Kyle and Crossley demonstrate that fine-grained indices distinguish types of syntactic complexity [R39]. | Sentence length or complexity alone does not predict quality. A short sentence can carry a crucial result; a long sentence can be precise. | Rebuild syntax when it obscures semantic relations, repeats a template, or overloads working memory. Do not vary grammar mechanically. |
| **5. Information progression** | Sentences introduce, maintain, and develop entities and propositions so that new information is connected to what the reader already knows. | Entity-grid coherence models formalise transitions among discourse entities [R33]; rhetorical-status work distinguishes contribution, background, and support [R34]. | Local coherence measures are imperfect and do not replace disciplinary interpretation. | Make the topic, evidence, explanation, and consequence visible in the order most useful for the audience. |
| **6. Paragraph-level development** | A paragraph develops a controlling function through a topic or claim, support, explanation/interpretation, and a relation to the next move. | Genre and multidimensional corpus studies show that academic assignments vary by discipline, level, genre, and informational density [R28–R30]. | Not every paragraph requires an explicit topic sentence or fixed four-part pattern; methods and results often use different structures. | Improve progression at the level of argument, not by forcing a universal paragraph template. |
| **7. Contextual cohesion and coherence** | Cohesion uses grammatical, lexical, referential, and connective resources; coherence is the reader’s construction of meaningful relations among propositions. | Metadiscourse research treats reader guidance as contextual and multifunctional [R21]; entity-based work models local coherence [R33]. | A connector can be redundant or can carry essential logic; surface cohesion cannot compensate for a missing argument. | Preserve or add explicit links only when the logical relation is genuine and useful. Check neighbouring sentences, not isolated connectives. |
| **8. Rhetorical moves and genre adaptation** | Writers perform socially recognisable moves such as establishing a territory, identifying a gap, describing methods, presenting results, and interpreting implications. | Genre families in BAWE and multidimensional analyses show that writing varies across discipline, level, and genre [R28–R31]. Teufel and Moens model rhetorical status in scientific articles [R34]. | Rhetorical move sequences differ across fields and sections; there is no universal IMRaD template for every text. | Identify the requested genre and preserve its legitimate moves; do not insert literature-review language into methods or discussion language into results without justification. |
| **9. Stance and calibrated commitment** | Writers express certainty, probability, evaluation, alignment, and distance through modals, reporting verbs, evaluative language, and other stance resources. | Hyland and Jiang document disciplinary and diachronic variation in stance/metadiscourse [R21–R24]; citation work links reporting verbs to source positioning [R32, R42]. | “More hedging” is not inherently more academic. Over-hedging weakens supported claims; under-hedging overstates evidence. | Match commitment to design, evidence, uncertainty, and disciplinary convention. Preserve the writer’s intended position. |
| **10. Engagement and reader awareness** | Writers anticipate readers’ knowledge, objections, and processing needs through transitions, code glosses, questions, directives, and strategic explanation. | Hyland defines metadiscourse as commentary that helps readers organise, interpret, and evaluate a text; its categories are context-dependent [R21]. | The same form can be propositional or metadiscoursal; frequency counts without contextual coding are unreliable [R21]. | Improve reader guidance when it clarifies a difficult relationship or term; avoid adding generic “this paper will…” framing by default. |
| **11. Evidence integration and citation framing** | A citation is integrated as a source, support, contrast, method, definition, or object of critique, with an appropriate reporting verb and interpretation. | BAWE research shows varied citation purposes across disciplines and levels, beyond simplified student-guide patterns [R32]. Citation frames influence how scientific contributions are positioned [R42]. | Correct form depends on source type, discipline, and local style; automated claim-source checking is limited without source access. | Keep citations and numerical details. Make the source–claim relation explicit only when supported by the original text or supplied source. |
| **12. Authorial voice and identity** | Voice is the patterned expression of perspective, evaluation, self-positioning, rhythm, and lexical/syntactic preference within a social genre. | Genre, discipline, and identity are interrelated in academic writing [R24]; multilingual scholars’ writing is mediated through complex institutional and editorial practices [R35]. Comparative AI/human work also highlights authorship and voice as distinct concerns [R15]. | Voice is not a fixed fingerprint; it changes across genres, collaborators, languages, audiences, and editing stages. | Preserve the source writer’s perspective and preferences. Do not add personal stories, opinions, or artificial idiosyncrasies. |
| **13. Disciplinary and proficiency variation** | Natural writing reflects different epistemologies, argument traditions, terminology, reader expectations, and language resources. | BAWE studies map variation by discipline, level, and genre [R28–R30]. L2/L1 bundle and academic-language studies document proficiency and educational variation [R27, R38, R39]. | A corpus norm is not a universal rule; “native-like” is not the same as expert or appropriate. | Ask for or infer discipline and audience cautiously. Treat non-native English as a legitimate authorial context, not a defect to erase. |
| **14. Revision and imperfection with purpose** | Human texts may contain local repetition, unevenness, or conventional wording because writers draft, revise, negotiate meaning, and respond to constraints. | Multilingual academic-writing research describes mediation and institutional processes rather than a simple native/non-native binary [R35]. Writing-assistant research emphasises user, task, interaction, and ecosystem dimensions [R40, R41]. | Poor grammar or ambiguity may still impede communication and should be corrected when requested. | Correct genuine errors and improve clarity, but do not manufacture mistakes or preserve avoidable confusion to simulate humanity. |

## B3. Corpus interpretation principles

### B3.1 BAWE and student-writing corpora

The BAWE corpus was designed from assessed undergraduate and taught-postgraduate texts across disciplines, with inclusion based on disciplinary assessment rather than only first-language status [R30]. Its genre families and multidimensional analyses show that “academic writing” is internally heterogeneous [R28, R29]. A humaniser may use such evidence to avoid one-size-fits-all rules, not to copy phrases or impose BAWE frequencies on research articles.

### B3.2 Published research articles and professional genres

Published research articles involve community-specific rhetorical choices, citation practices, stance, and competition for publication [R22–R24, R31, R42]. A thesis introduction may contain more previews and chapter references than a journal article introduction [R31]. Therefore transformation must identify the section and genre before changing metadiscourse.

### B3.3 L1/L2 and multilingual writing

L1/L2 differences in lexical bundles, syntax, and academic language reflect proficiency, exposure, task, discipline, and educational context; they are not evidence of deficient thinking or artificial authorship [R27, R35, R38, R39]. Editing should improve communicative effectiveness while respecting the writer’s meaning and identity. The fairness risks of AI detection against non-native writers reinforce this safeguard [R12].

## B4. Naturalness is relational, not statistical

A passage can be natural even when it has high lexical repetition, long sentences, passive voice, conventional transitions, or low stylistic novelty if those properties are motivated by its discipline and purpose. Conversely, a passage can have varied vocabulary and sentence lengths yet remain unnatural if references are unclear, evidence is disconnected, or claims are overstated. The editorial target is therefore **functional variation plus semantic and rhetorical control**, not maximal distributional distance from a language model.

## B5. Operational principles derived from Framework B

1. Diagnose the relationship between wording and communicative function.
2. Preserve technical terms and key entities across a paragraph.
3. Use sentence variation to express logical relations, not to satisfy a detector heuristic.
4. Treat stance, hedging, engagement, and citation as contextual discourse functions.
5. Adapt editing to discipline, section, genre, audience, and proficiency.
6. Strengthen explanation and interpretation only when the source supports them.
7. Preserve legitimate authorial presence and avoid a generic “academic” voice.
8. Evaluate the whole paragraph and argument, not isolated phrases.
